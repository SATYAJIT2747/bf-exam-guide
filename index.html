1. Finance ratios: Section I ASKED 2025

Source: Finance 101 (Ruchie Gupta). Last year this was a separate 20-mark section. This year it may come inside a case, so memorise every formula and benchmark below.

1.1 The three statements in one breath

Income Statement (P&L)
Profit over a period.
Revenue − Costs = Profit
Balance Sheet
Snapshot at a moment.
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Cash Flow Statement
Where cash came from and went: Operating, Investing, Financing.
TermPlain meaning
RevenueMoney from selling goods/services in normal operations
OpExRunning costs (salary, marketing, support). Hits profit now.
CapExBuying long-life assets (machines, property, capitalised software). Spread over years via depreciation.
EBITDARevenue − OpEx = operating profit before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, Amortisation. EBITDA margin = EBITDA / Revenue. Slide example: 39/100 = 39%.
Net IncomeBottom-line profit after everything
Free Cash FlowNet income ± non-cash items − CapEx. Real cash left to reinvest or return. Slide: 24 + 12 − 8 = 28.
Assets / Liabilities / EquityWhat you own / what you owe / the owner's stake (capital + retained earnings)
Burn rateCash used per month. Runway = Cash ÷ monthly burn.
Budget vs ForecastBudget = plan for allocating resources. Forecast = prediction from past + trends.
Profit is opinion, cash is fact. A startup can be profitable and still unable to pay salaries if customers pay in 90 days (Idea to Venture slide: cash fell from 50 lakh to 4 lakh while profitable). Growth eats cash. Levers: collect faster, invoice upfront, delay payables, use a credit line, factor receivables.

1.3 Worked: Infosys FY24 (last year's exact question)

Revenue 153,670 · Net Profit 26,248 · Total Assets 137,814 · CA 89,432 · CL 38,794 · Receivables 30,193 (₹ crore)

Q1 Liquidity
Current ratio = 89,432 / 38,794 = 2.31. Infosys has ₹2.31 of short-term assets for every ₹1 of short-term debt, so it can comfortably pay. Working capital = ₹50,638 cr.
Stress test: even after removing receivables, (89,432 − 30,193) / 38,794 = 1.53, still above 1. An IT firm has almost no inventory, so the quick ratio ≈ the current ratio.
Interpretation: very strong liquidity, above the 1.5–2 healthy band. It could even be called slightly conservative (cash not deployed). Watch receivables: DSO = 30,193/153,670 × 365 ≈ 72 days.
Q2 Profitability
Net profit margin = 26,248 / 153,670 = 17.1%. It keeps ~₹0.17 from every ₹1 of sales. That is above the general 10% benchmark and in line with or above the IT-services peer band (~15–20%).
Q3 Return on assets
ROA = 26,248 / 137,814 = 19.0%. It earns ₹19 for every ₹100 of assets. This is well above the 5–10% norm because IT services is asset-light (people, not factories). Asset turnover = 1.12×.
Q4 Cost-to-income
Total costs = 153,670 − 26,248 = 127,422 → C/I = 127,422 / 153,670 = 82.9%. Assumption: only net profit is given, so all costs incl. tax are counted.
Margins: a 17% net margin is healthy and high for a services business, but not product-software level (25%+), because people costs dominate.

2. Business case & investment math ASKED 2025

Sources: Finance 101 (Business case, CBA, Cloud & Token economics) and Idea to Venture Part 2.

2.1 What goes in a business case

Finance 101 components (memorise the order)
  1. Executive summary
  2. Problem statement
  3. Proposed solution
  4. Benefits analysis (tangible + intangible)
  5. Cost-benefit analysis (NPV/IRR)
  6. Risk assessment + mitigation
  7. Implementation plan (timeline, milestones)
APM five-case model (Idea to Venture)
  1. Strategic case: why change, why now
  2. Economic case: ROI of the options
  3. Commercial case: sourcing/procurement
  4. Financial case: affordability
  5. Management case: governance, roles

Horizon's paper was structured exactly like this (Strategic → Economic → Risk).

Why business cases fail (good for critique questions): baseline costs hard to estimate · migration costs underestimated · benefits overstated · over-emphasis on money · "pet project" bias · forgotten after approval. Fix: benefits realisation, meaning each benefit gets an owner and is tracked after go-live.

2.2 Tangible vs intangible benefits

Tangible (count in ₹, hours, units)
Automation, fewer manual hours, faster month-end close, lower IT maintenance, grow volume without adding staff, lower recruitment/training cost.
Intangible (real, hard to number; still list them)
Better customer/user experience, less employee stress/burnout, business agility (launch faster), reputation with regulator, brand.

Slide NPV example: −100,000 now; +3,710, +3,710, +103,710 discounted at 5% → 3,533 + 3,365 + 89,589 − 100,000 = NPV −3,513 → reject.

NPV vs Real Options (very examinable for AI/PoC cases)
Project cost $100M. 25% chance of success (benefit $250M), 75% chance of failure ($30M).
NPV lens: 0.25×250 + 0.75×30 − 100 = −$15M → reject.
Option lens: run a pilot first and invest only if it succeeds: 0.25 × max(250−100, 0) + 0.75 × 0 = +$37.5M.
So a pilot/PoC is worth paying for (up to ~$37.5M + 15M ≈ $52.5M of value difference). Lesson: under uncertainty, stage the investment (pilot → scale). That keeps the option open. "Tomato garden" (Luehrman): some options should be harvested now, some kept to ripen, some never.

2.4 TCO: total cost of ownership

TCO = acquisition + ownership (run) + exit. Proposals price only the visible tip.

TCO of AI is an iceberg
Visible: licences, API tokens, cloud GPUs.
Hidden: data cleaning/labelling, integration with core systems and security, assurance (evaluation, guardrails, red-teaming, audit), people (engineers, change management, retraining), run costs (monitoring, model drift, upgrades, support).
Classic TCO list: hardware/software, storage/security, data migration, resources, retraining, change management.

2.5 Cloud & token economics LIKELY

Cloud cost lines: Compute (per hour/request), Storage (per GB/month), Data transfer/egress (most overlooked), Managed services.
Levers: right-size, auto-scale and shut down idle, reserved/spot pricing, tag and monitor spend.
Token cost lines: input tokens, output tokens (2–4× pricier), model tier (big model 10–20× cost), context/memory (chat history resent every call).
Levers: right-size model, cache and reuse, limits and alerts, trim prompts.
2× conversation length → ~4–5× tokens · RAG → +2–5× tokens per query · token prices fall ~10× every 1–2 years but usage rises faster.

2.6 Make vs buy maths (slide examples)

  • Make vs Buy: In-house = $200k capex + (50+30+20) = $100k/yr. Buy = $130k/yr. Making saves $30k/yr → payback 200/30 ≈ 6.7 years. Buy unless you need it for >7 years or it is strategic.
  • New machine: net cost = 350 + 75 − 10 salvage = $415k; savings $40k/yr → payback ≈ 10.4 years on savings alone, so the revenue upside (+5% capacity, better quality) must justify it.
  • App launch: upfront $750k. Margin per user = $0.99 − $0.50 cloud = $0.49/month (before store fees). 50,000 users → $294k/yr → payback ~2.5–3.5 yrs. Cloud cost per user is the key sensitivity.

2.7 Model answers: 2025 Case 1, Horizon Holidays ASKED 2025

Concepts you need (not all are on slides, but they were asked)
  • Technical debt: the hidden "interest" you pay for keeping an old, easy-for-now system: maintenance + slow change + scarce skills.
  • Cost of Inaction (COI): what doing nothing costs = running cost + lost revenue (cost of delay) + risk cost.
  • Sunk cost fallacy: "it's fully depreciated, so it's free" is wrong. Depreciation is an accounting entry; the $15M/yr is real cash.
  • Strangler Fig / Wrap & Extend: build a new layer around the old system, move functions over piece by piece, and retire the old core later. The opposite is Rip & Replace (big-bang rewrite: high risk, like the TSB migration failure).
  • Deterministic vs probabilistic: mainframe gives the same answer every time; an LLM can guess (hallucinate).
  • Human-in-the-loop (HITL): a human approves or audits AI actions before or after execution.
Q1 Cost of Inaction (4)
  • "Costs nothing" is a sunk-cost illusion. Cash cost = $15M/yr just to keep the lights on. That is the technical-debt interest, paid forever, and rising as COBOL talent retires.
  • Cost of delay: 9 months vs competitors' 2 weeks → ~8.5 months during which ESG-focused corporate clients see a rival offer a Sustainability Score and Horizon cannot. Assume 5% of $300M revenue depends on such features → $15M/yr at risk → 8.5/12 × 15 ≈ $10.6M lost per feature cycle, plus contracts lost in RFPs are lost for multi-year terms.
  • COI per year ≈ $15M + ~$10M + attrition cost (Q2) ≈ $25–28M > $20M one-time investment. Doing nothing is the expensive option.
  • Strategy link: agility is now a competitive requirement (operational effectiveness). Slow feature speed = lost market share (VUCA: volatility needs slack and speed).
Q2 Talent risk (6)
  • Problem: 40% attrition + 6 weeks training on cryptic codes = constant hire-train-lose cycle and lost tacit knowledge.
  • How Agentic AI helps: agents type in natural language and the AI translates to mainframe codes → training drops from 6 weeks to ~1 week, the job gets less frustrating (lower attrition), new hires are productive sooner, and AI handles 60% of the routine changes so humans do higher-value work.
  • Illustrative numbers: 500 agents × 40% = 200 leavers/yr × ~$11k (recruiting $5k + 6 weeks paid training ~$6k) = $2.2M/yr. With AI: attrition 20%, training 1 week → 100 × ~$6k = $0.6M. Saving ≈ $1.6M/yr.
  • Both, but book them separately: recruitment and training savings are a direct, tangible financial saving (goes in the NPV). Better morale, customer experience and knowledge retention are an operational / intangible benefit (listed, not double-counted).
  • Change management: use Lewin's Unfreeze → Change → Refreeze, train champions, and frame AI as the agent's assistant, not their replacement (adaptive challenge, people resist loss).
Q3 ROI of Strangler Fig vs Rip & Replace (5)
Rip & ReplaceWrap & Extend (AI layer)

Benefits within 3 yrsZero. Still building in year 3 (~$60M spent) and still paying $15M/yr maintenance on the old system meanwhile.Start in year 2: transaction savings $7.2M (Q4) + training/attrition $1.6M + faster features + reduced change requests on COBOL
Illustrative cash flows ($M)−20, −20, −20, 0 → NPV@10% ≈ −$55M; IRR negative−20, +10, +15, +15 → NPV@10% ≈ +$12.8M, IRR ≈ 41%

Maintenance that remains: mainframe hosting/MIPS and licences, a smaller COBOL core team (the mainframe stays the system of record), plus new AI run costs (tokens, monitoring, model upgrades, guardrails; see the TCO iceberg).
Eliminated or reduced: most new-feature coding in COBOL (built in the AI/API layer instead), green-screen training, manual labour on 60% of transactions, part of the scarce-COBOL premium.
Why IRR is better: IRR rewards early cash flows and smaller investment. Wrap pays back in ~2 years; Rip pays nothing within the 3-year horizon.
Q4 Unit economics (4)
AI handles 60% × 1,000,000 = 600,000 transactions.
Gross saving = 600,000 × $12 = $7.2M per year.
Net of AI cost (assume ~$0.50/transaction in tokens and compute) = 600,000 × 0.50 = $0.3M → net ≈ $6.9M/yr.
New blended cost/transaction = (400,000 × 12 + 600,000 × 0.5) / 1M = $5.10 (down 57.5% from $12).
Speed: 15 min → 30 sec = 30× faster. Agent time freed = 600,000 × 15 min = 150,000 hours ≈ 75 FTEs (at 2,000 hrs/yr), who can be redeployed to complex cases.
Payback on $20M ≈ 20 / 6.9 ≈ 2.9 years from this lever alone.
Q5 Hallucination risk + HITL governance (6)
Mitigation: make the AI propose and the mainframe decide.
  • Grounding / read-back check: the AI may only book flights that exist in a live mainframe query. Before confirming, it re-reads the green screen and verifies flight number, date, fare and PNR. Any mismatch → block. This is poka-yoke for AI.
  • Constrained actions: the AI can only call a whitelist of mainframe commands, with no free-text writes. Read-only by default.
  • Confidence thresholds: low confidence or high value (e.g. >$5k, VIP, multi-leg international) → route to a human.
  • Audit trail: every prompt, command and response is logged. Control charts track error rate (common vs special cause).
  • Staged rollout (CrowdStrike lesson: canary, not big bang) and a kill switch.

Named accountable owner, because you can outsource the work but not the accountability. If the AI is from a vendor, the SLA should carry accuracy targets and liability carve-outs.

3. Strategy & Porter's Five Forces ASKED 2025

Source: "What is your strategy" (Dr. Warudkar, Session 3).

3.1 What strategy is

  • Strategy = winning through competitive advantage: a unique position from a system of activities across the value chain. It's about doing it differently, not just better. It's about choices and trade-offs.
  • Porter (1996): operational effectiveness (best practice) is not strategy, because everyone copies it. Strategy is what you choose NOT to do. No trade-off = nothing to defend.
  • Competitive advantage = better value than rivals via better quality, convenience or lower price. Value creation = profits = revenue ↑ − costs ↓.
Mintzberg's 5 Ps: "Plan, Ploy, Pattern, Position, Perspective"
PMeaningDiagnostic question
Planintended course, decided in advanceWhat did we say we'd do?
Ploya manoeuvre to outwit a rivalWhat are we doing to move them?
Patternconsistency in actual behaviourWhat do we actually keep doing?
Positionfit between firm and environmentWhere do we stand?
Perspectiveingrained way of seeing / cultureWho do we think we are?
Deliberate + Emergent (Mintzberg & Waters): realised strategy = intended plans carried out + patterns that formed unplanned. If plan and spending differ, the pattern is the real strategy.

3.2 Porter's Five Forces: the full drivers

Purpose: how big and defensible is the industry profit pool? Strong forces mean low profit.

ForceIt's HIGH when…
Supplier powerconcentrated suppliers · unique inputs · high switching costs · supplier can forward-integrate
Buyer powerfew, large, informed buyers · undifferentiated product · low switching cost · buyer can backward-integrate
Threat of new entrantslow capital needs · no scale economies · no switching costs · no policy/regulatory barrier
Threat of substitutesattractive price-performance from outside the industry · low switching cost
Rivalrymany equal rivals · high fixed costs · slow growth · high exit barriers · commodity product
Refinements the professor stresses (bonus marks)
  • Define the industry first. The boundary decides the answer (e.g. "foundation-model layer" vs "AI applications").
  • Industry growth is NOT a force. Fast growth invites entry; it doesn't guarantee profit ("who captures the $44bn?").
  • Read the direction, not the level. Say where each force is heading (↑/↓).
  • Limits: government can be a 6th player (regulator + customer + competitor + supplier, as in Indian space); there is no word for complementors (who grow the pie); the model is static; structure doesn't explain why one firm wins (use VRIO for that).

Space case verdict style to copy: "Launch: rivalry HIGH, buyers HIGH, substitutes HIGH → structurally unattractive. Satcom: entry LOW (regulation) but substitutes VERY HIGH (cheap 4G/5G) → attractive on barriers, unattractive on substitutes."

3.3 Resource-Based View: VRIO LIKELY

TestQuestionIf it fails here →
ValuableRaises value or cuts cost?competitive disadvantage
RareOnly a few firms have it?competitive parity
InimitableCostly to copy/substitute?temporary advantage
OrganisedIs the firm set up to exploit it?unused advantage

All four → sustained competitive advantage. Hard-to-copy resources are usually socially complex, path-dependent, tacit (e.g. Jersey Mike's franchisee network, Toyota culture), not the product itself.

3.4 Other strategy tools

Blue Ocean / value innovation LIKELY
Escape the price war by breaking the value-cost trade-off. ERRC grid: Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create. Strategy canvas = a different curve, not a higher one. Weakness: picks success stories after the fact.
Disruption (Christensen), stated precisely
Incumbents overshoot their best customers → entrant takes a low-end/new-market foothold (worse on the old metric, better on price/access) → incumbents rationally ignore it → entrant moves upmarket. Not every successful entrant (Uber is not disruptive by Christensen's definition).
Ecosystems / co-opetition
Complementors grow the pie (Brandenburger & Nalebuff). Adner: ecosystems carry co-innovation risk and adoption-chain risk.
Dynamic capabilities (Teece) LIKELY
Ability to integrate, build, reconfigure competences in fast-changing environments. Three parts: Sensing (spot opportunities and threats) → Seizing (mobilise resources) → Transforming (renew the organisation). Ordinary capabilities = doing things right (easy to copy). Dynamic = doing the right things (hard to copy). Repurposing an organisation is harder than repurposing a technology.

Evolution: 1980s Five Forces (industry decides) → 1990s RBV (VRIN resources decide) → 2000+ Dynamic Capabilities (orchestration under deep uncertainty decides).

Low-cost airline model: one plane type, point-to-point, one class, no meals or assigned seats. Remember: low cost ≠ low price. Low cost is a structural position; low price is just a tactic.

3.5 Model answers: 2025 Case 2, Apex Intelligence (VC deciding) ASKED 2025

Industry definition: general-purpose foundation-model layer, not AI applications.

Force 1, Supplier power: VERY HIGH ↑ (Q1, Q2)
  • Chain of chokepoints: NVIDIA (~80–90% of AI accelerators, plus CUDA software lock-in = huge switching cost) → TSMC (makes nearly all advanced chips and packaging) → AI startups. Every Porter supplier-power driver is present: concentrated, unique input, high switching cost, and forward integration (NVIDIA's own cloud and investments in favoured labs).
  • Apex is a tiny buyer: no volume, no leverage. Hyperscalers (Microsoft, Amazon, Google) get allocation first; Apex waits 9 months or signs a cloud deal and becomes dependent on a rival's partner.
  • Kingmaker? Largely yes in the short term. Whoever gets GPU allocation can train, and whoever can't falls a generation behind. Direction: weakening slowly (AMD MI300, Google TPU, AWS Trainium), but not within Apex's runway.
  • Margins vs 2015 SaaS: SaaS gross margin ~75–85% because serving one more user costs ~nothing and cloud was a commodity. AI model firms have ~40–60% gross margin because compute is a large COGS (training capex + inference per token). Value is captured by the supplier (NVIDIA's own gross margin is ~75%).
  • Kraljic: for Apex, GPUs are a Strategic item (high impact, high risk). They need long-term partnership or a reserved-capacity contract, not spot buying.
Force 2, Threat of new entrants: LOW for the frontier, HIGH for niches (Q3)
  • Capital moat: frontier training costs $100M+ in compute alone, plus data, talent and scale economies. The garage era is over for frontier foundation models, but alive at the application layer.
  • Apex $50M vs OpenAI $10B = 200× less. That isn't even one frontier training run. It cannot realistically win on general model quality.
  • Pivot to a vertical niche (Porter's focus strategy), e.g. AI for Law in the EU: proprietary legal data, compliance, domain evals, where general models are "good enough" but not trusted.
  • Its real VRIO asset is the 30% energy-efficient architecture: valuable and rare, but imitable within months, so a temporary advantage. Use it as a cost advantage on inference, or license it.
Force 3, Threat of substitutes: VERY HIGH ↑ (Q4)
  • Free open-source models (Llama-4) "almost as good" set a price ceiling: Apex can charge at most the cost of self-hosting a free model plus the hassle of doing so.
  • Meta's motive: commoditise the complement. Meta earns from ads, not models, so making models free hurts rivals' pricing power.
  • Why would anyone still pay Apex? "Free" isn't free (TCO iceberg): GPUs, MLOps engineers, security, monitoring, upgrades. Apex can sell a managed service + SLA + copyright indemnity (the NYT/Getty lawsuits make this valuable) + EU data residency/GDPR + better accuracy in one vertical + lower energy cost per token.
Force 4, Buyer power: HIGH ↑ (Q5)
  • LangChain-style tools make models swappable with one line of code. Switching cost ≈ 0, buyers are large and informed, and the product is undifferentiated, so yes, the model layer is commoditising.
  • Build stickiness (raise switching cost): fine-tune on the customer's own data (asset specificity) · deep workflow integrations and APIs · proprietary evaluation and guardrails tuned to their domain · compliance certifications and EU hosting · copyright and output indemnity · enterprise SLA and support · multi-year volume contracts or outcome-based pricing · data feedback loop that makes the model better for that customer over time (Hook-model "investment").
Force 5, Rivalry: EXTREME ↑ (Q6)
  • Microsoft and Google bundle AI into Office/Workspace as a loss leader to protect their core monopolies. They can price at zero indefinitely; Apex can't.
  • Survival playbook: (1) Don't fight on price. Use Blue Ocean ERRC: eliminate general chat, reduce model size, raise domain accuracy and compliance, create indemnified vertical workflows. (2) Go where Big Tech won't: regulated verticals, sovereign European AI. (3) Be a complementor, not a competitor: sell through Azure/AWS marketplaces (co-opetition). (4) Use the efficiency edge for the lowest cost per token. (5) License the architecture or position as an acquisition target.
VC verdict
All five forces are high, so the foundation-model layer is structurally unattractive for a $50M entrant (like Indian launch services). Do not invest in the "compete with OpenAI" thesis. Invest only if Apex pivots to a vertical niche or efficiency licensing, with a compute partnership secured upfront.

4. Customer-centric product design ASKED 2025 (30 marks)

Source: Customer Centric Product Design (Mahesh Natarajan). This was the biggest case last year.

4.1 Why customer-centric

Product/Tech-driven (Market PUSH)
"Cool feature → find customers." High risk, slower to market. Suited to breakthrough projects.
Customer-centric (Market PULL)
Start with customer needs → minimal features for adoption. Lower risk, faster. Examples: Heinz upside-down bottle, simple TV remote, Paytm Soundbox (audio confirmation in noisy shops).

Innovation triad: Design Thinking (frame the human problem) + Lean Startup (test hypotheses fast, MVP) + Agile (ship small increments). 100% Agile with 0% Design Thinking and 0% Lean = an unguided "feature factory".

CCPD path (4 steps): "Charter → Insight → Prioritise → QFD"
  1. Program Charter: mission, scope, target market, right customer (tools: segmentation, A3, multi-generation plan)
  2. Customer Insight / VOC: surveys, interviews, focus groups, ethnography, conjoint analysis
  3. Prioritise needs: Affinity diagram → Structured tree → Kano
  4. Translate to specs: QFD / House of Quality

4.2 Voice of Customer (VOC) methods

MethodBest for
Lead-user analysisfront-end innovation, finding opportunities
Customer advisory councilB2B feedback, testing use cases with key customers
Participatory designco-developing solutions with customers
Ethnography (observe in real life)unarticulated needs, how the product fits daily workflow
Focus groups / interviews / surveysstated needs, testing hypotheses

Qualitative (why: interviews, ethnography; in-depth but subjective) vs Quantitative (how many: surveys, analytics; objective but lacks context).

Conjoint analysis = "features CONsidered JOINTly". Show realistic bundles and force trade-offs (₹0 fee + no rewards vs ₹10k fee + 5% cashback) to infer what customers truly value (part-worth utilities). Why: when asked directly, people rate everything as "very important" (low discrimination). This gives revealed preference over stated preference.

Affinity diagram = group many raw needs into themes. Structured tree = break a theme into sub-needs. Then Kano to prioritise.

4.4 QFD / House of Quality ASKED 2025

  • Purpose: translate vague customer WHATs into measurable technical HOWs before design is locked, which avoids costly redesign later.
  • Parts of the house: left wall = customer needs (WHATs) + importance (1–5) · ceiling = technical descriptors (HOWs) · middle = relationship matrix · roof = correlations between HOWs (conflict or synergy) · right = competitor comparison · basement = target values + technical importance.
  • Scoring: Strong = 9, Medium = 3, Weak = 1. Technical importance of a HOW = Σ (need importance × relationship score). Highest score = most critical HOW, the one to protect or invest in first.
  • Cascades into 4 houses: customer needs → functional requirements → part characteristics → manufacturing processes → process controls.

4.5 Hook Model (Nir Eyal) ASKED 2025

Not on the CCPD slides you have, but it was asked, so learn it.

StepMeaning
1. TriggerWhat prompts use. External (notification, message) → later internal (an emotion: anxiety, boredom, curiosity).
2. ActionThe simplest behaviour done in anticipation of a reward (one tap). Fogg: Behaviour = Motivation + Ability + Trigger.
3. Variable rewardUnpredictable reward that keeps people coming back. Three types: Tribe (social), Hunt (information/resources), Self (mastery, competence).
4. InvestmentUser puts something in (data, effort, content) that improves the product and loads the next trigger. This creates switching cost.

5. Quality management LIKELY

5.1 Defining quality (Garvin)

5 lenses (1984)
  • Transcendent: "I know it when I see it" (Taj Hotel)
  • Product-based: measurable attribute, more is better (megapixels)
  • User-based: fitness for use (Alto for a first-time buyer)
  • Manufacturing-based: conformance, right first time (Six Sigma)
  • Value-based: performance at an acceptable price (Jio, IndiGo)
8 dimensions (1987): compete on a few, not all
P-F-R-C-D-S-A-P: Performance, Features, Reliability, Conformance, Durability, Serviceability, Aesthetics, Perceived quality.
Mnemonic: "Please Find Real Customers During Service, Always Pleased".
The 99.9% trap. UPI has ~698M transactions/day. 99.9% success still means ~6.98 lakh failures/day. Six Sigma (3.4 per million) gives ~2,400/day. At scale, 99.9% is a crisis. Use this line in any AI/tech-at-scale answer.

5.5 Cases: one-line lessons

  • Mumbai dabbawalas: near-zero errors with no IT, through standardisation, colour codes (poka-yoke) and culture.
  • UPI April 2025 outage (~5 hrs): banks flooded "check status" calls. System quality = the weakest participant's conformance. Fix: rate limits and API SLAs.
  • Toyota 2009–10 recall: "grew faster than our people". Quality capability scales slowly.
  • Boeing 737 MAX-9: four bolts never installed. Both appraisal and prevention failed, under culture and schedule pressure.
  • CrowdStrike 2024: one config update crashed 8.5M machines. Fix: canary/staged rollouts.
  • Knight Capital 2012: $460M lost in 45 min from one un-updated server and no deployment procedure.
  • Ranbaxy 2013: data-integrity fraud, $500M. Appraisal you can't trust is worse than none.
  • Quality pays: award winners saw +107% operating income growth (Hendricks & Singhal). But "announcing" Six Sigma ≠ capability, and process focus can kill exploration (Benner & Tushman).

6. Vendor management & contracts LIKELY

Source: Vendor Management and Contracts, Session 7. A natural case topic (Apex and NVIDIA, Horizon's AI vendor, outsourcing).

6.1 Make, buy or ally? Transaction Cost Economics (Williamson)

Three drivers: Asset specificity (investment useless outside this relationship), Uncertainty (hard to specify or measure), Frequency. The higher all three are, the more you move from Market (buy) → Hybrid (ally/JV) → Hierarchy (make).

Boeing–Spirit: fuselages = high specificity and quality-critical, so outsourcing struggled and Boeing bought Spirit back for $8.3B (2025). You can outsource the work, not the accountability.

6.2 Kraljic matrix LIKELY

Low supply riskHigh supply risk

Test: "How many credible suppliers could replace this one in 90 days?" High spend ≠ strategic.

6.3 Selection

  • Dickson (1966): price ranked only 6th, behind quality, delivery, performance history, warranties, capacity.
  • TCO = acquisition + ownership + exit. Hidden: onboarding, integration, audits, quality failures, downtime, renewal escalation, switching cost (AT&T: $40–50M to leave VMware).
  • 6 steps: define need → RFI → RFP/RFQ → evaluate (weighted scoring; L1 lowest bid vs QCBS quality-and-cost) → due diligence → negotiate (know your BATNA) and award.
  • Winner's curse: a bid far below the others comes back as change requests.
  • Weighted scoring (hospital cloud slide): CFO weights → Y 7.95, Z 7.05, X 7.0 (low-cost wins). CIO weights → Z 7.75, X 7.55, Y 7.3 (niche wins). The weights decide the winner, so fix them before the bids arrive.

6.4 Contracts

Stack: NDA/LOI → MSA (umbrella legal terms: liability, IP, data, termination) → SOW (per project: scope, deliverables, price, acceptance) → schedules (SLA, pricing, security, exit plan) → change requests.

10 clauses that bite: scope · acceptance · change control · price and payment · service levels · warranties and indemnities · limitation of liability · data/security/IP · subcontracting and change of control · termination and exit.
CrowdStrike vs Delta: Delta lost ~$500M but could recover only "single-digit millions", because liability was capped (~12 months' fees) and indirect/consequential losses were excluded. Only the carve-outs (gross negligence, wilful misconduct, data breach, IP indemnity) are uncapped. Lesson: read the liability clause first.

India law quick list: Contract Act s.73 (compensation for natural/foreseen loss only), s.74 (liquidated damages, reasonable up to the stated sum), Specific Relief Amendment 2018, Arbitration Act 1996, MSMED (pay small suppliers within 45 days), DPDP Act 2023 (data fiduciary responsible for its processors).

Case cards (one line each)

  • IndiGo–Pratt & Whitney: 70+ jets grounded. With a bottleneck/strategic supplier, negotiate compensation rather than litigate.

10. Innovation → venture LIKELY

Invention vs Innovation
Invention = creating something new (Spangler's vacuum). Innovation = its successful commercial adoption (Hoover). Innovation = economic application of invention.
Schumpeter: creative destruction
New combinations destroy old ways. 5 types: new good (iPhone) · new method (Ford assembly line) · new market (Bajaj in Africa) · new source of supply (lab-grown diamonds) · new organisation of the industry (Amul cooperative; UPI open network).
Diffusion of innovation (Rogers) + the Chasm (Moore)
Innovators 2.5% → Early adopters 13.5% → ‖ CHASM ‖ → Early majority 34% → Late majority 34% → Laggards 16%. Cumulative adoption is an S-curve.
The chasm: early adopters buy vision, the early majority buys proof, references and complete solutions. Example: Bhasha-Setu must win pragmatic teachers and parents with proof (English progress), not tech novelty.
  • Open innovation (Chesbrough): outside-in (bring in external ideas) and inside-out (license unused ideas out).
  • Market timing: first mover vs better late entrant. "The best is the enemy of the good."
  • 8 dimensions where a venture breaks: people, processes, technology, finance, legal, vendor management, quality, design. The course is built around these.
  • Startup journey: idea → team → structure → fund and register → build (prototype) → launch → scale.
  • Company structures (India): sole proprietorship (unlimited liability) · partnership (1932 Act) · LLP (2008 Act, limited liability) · Private Ltd (min 2 members; slides say max 50, Companies Act 2013 says 200; no public shares; min capital ₹1 lakh per slide) · Public Ltd (7+ members, public shares).
  • Knowledge: tacit (know-how, sticky, "we know more than we can tell", Polanyi) vs explicit (know-that, leaks easily). Legacy COBOL experts hold tacit knowledge, which is a risk in Horizon-type cases.
  • Rethinking cycle (Adam Grant): humility → doubt → curiosity → discovery, versus the overconfidence cycle (pride → conviction → confirmation bias → validation).

  • Unit economics: $1.4m sales per store (~3× Subway), ~$515k build cost, ~16% store margin, ~42% cash-on-cash return, payback ~2 years. Growth funds itself because franchisees want a second store.
  • Three lenses on one firm:

    • Resources (VRIO): the real asset is the self-selected, high-return franchisee network. It is socially complex and path-dependent, so it can't be bought. The sandwich itself is copyable.
    • Governance (transaction cost economics): centralise brand, standards and supply; leave capital and labour to franchisees. A 16% margin makes cheating on quality irrational (vs 6%).
  • Day of Giving: "shared value" (strategy that also helps society) vs the Crane critique (it's just philanthropy relabelled).
  • Blackstone buyout to IPO: the business didn't change; only the ownership of the cash flows did. Up-C structure: Blackstone keeps voting control ("controlled company"). The 10× oversubscribed IPO still fell below its issue price on day one, and the category (restaurants) was blamed.

Indian space sector: Five Forces under regulation

  • FDI gradient as deliberate barriers: components 100% automatic (to weaken supplier power), satellites/data 74%, launch vehicles 49% (dual-use sensitivity is an intentional entry barrier).
  • Government as a 6th force: regulator (IN-SPACe), main customer, competitor (ISRO/NSIL), supplier and financier (₹1,000 cr VC fund) at once.
  • Prestige ≠ profit pool: 393 foreign satellites launched in a decade earned only a few hundred million dollars in total (about the price of 4–5 commercial heavy-lift missions). Growth to $44bn by 2033 doesn't tell you who captures it.

Aravind Eye Care / Narayana Health

High quality at low cost: huge volume, assembly-line standardised surgery, task-shifting to trained paramedics, surgeons doing only the surgery itself. Lesson: quality and cost can move together; the trade-off is often a design choice. Use it in any "affordable at scale in India" case.

Pricing (airlines and AI/digital products)

  • Airline dynamic pricing (revenue/yield management): price depends on route, seat class, slots left, time of day, season, aircraft, competition and passenger mix. Same seat, different prices by willingness to pay.
  • AI/digital product pricing options: per-seat subscription · usage/token-based · tiered/freemium · outcome-based. Price has to cover variable compute cost per user (token economics), unlike classic near-zero-cost software.

McKinsey: Eight Essentials of Innovation

Aspire (set bold targets) · Choose (pick a portfolio of bets) · Discover (insight into customer, technology, business model) · Evolve (business model, not just product) · Accelerate (beat bureaucracy) · Scale · Extend (use external networks / open innovation) · Mobilise (people and culture). Mnemonic: "A-C-D-E-A-S-E-M".

From the missing CCPD Part 2 (likely topics; learn the basics)

  • MVP (Lean Startup): smallest product that tests the riskiest assumption. Build → Measure → Learn. Pivot or persevere.
  • Value Proposition Canvas (Osterwalder): customer side = Jobs, Pains, Gains. Product side = Products/services, Pain relievers, Gain creators. "Fit" means the product side addresses the customer side.
  • Design Thinking stages: Empathise → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test.
  • Crossing the Chasm / Innovator's Dilemma / Blue Ocean appear on the reading list, and the guide already covers them.

Other image-only slides, one line each

  • Maslow: physiological → safety → belonging → esteem → self-actualisation (meet lower needs first).
  • Lean / Toyota Way: respect for people + continuous improvement; see Quality §5.4.
  • Business case for digital investments (flow): identify metrics → is there a baseline? (if not, create one) → quantify costs → quantify benefits → articulate intangible value.
